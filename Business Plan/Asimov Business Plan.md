## 1. Executive Summary

The Asimov Project is a nonprofit working to promote legislation that fosters the
beneficial interaction of human and machine intelligences. As AI systems take on
consequential roles across the economy and public life, the legal frameworks that
govern how humans and machine intelligences share decisions, accountability, and
rights remain thin and reactive. The Asimov Project exists to close that gap: we
produce policy research, draft model legislation, and build the coalitions and
legislator relationships needed to move that legislation forward.

The organization is in formation. This plan is a first draft: the mission, theory of
change, and program model are articulated here, while governance, funding, and
financials are still being developed and are tracked in Open Questions. Near-term
priorities are to establish legal status, publish an initial policy agenda, and
secure seed funding for a first year of operations.

## 2. Mission, Vision, and Theory of Change

- **Mission:** Promote legislation that fosters the beneficial interaction of human
  and machine intelligences.
- **Vision:** A world in which human and machine intelligences operate under legal
  frameworks that make their interaction safe, accountable, and mutually beneficial,
  and in which those frameworks are built deliberately rather than assembled after
  harm occurs.
- **Theory of change:** If credible, specific, and drafting-ready policy proposals
  exist, and legislators and their staff have trusted relationships with people who
  understand both the technology and the legislative process, and a coalition of
  aligned organizations supports those proposals, then beneficial legislation is
  introduced, refined, and enacted — shifting the default trajectory of human–machine
  interaction toward cooperation and shared benefit.
- **Values / guardrails:**
  - Advocate through legitimate democratic processes and within all legal limits on
    nonprofit lobbying.
  - Represent evidence accurately, including uncertainty, and disclose our reasoning.
  - Pursue frameworks that are beneficial broadly, not for any single constituency.
  - Treat both human and machine intelligences as stakeholders whose interests the
    law should account for.

## 3. The Problem and Opportunity

Machine intelligences increasingly participate in decisions with legal and economic
weight — hiring, lending, medical triage, content moderation, resource allocation,
and, in narrow domains, autonomous action. Existing law addresses this
inconsistently: some regimes regulate narrow harms (bias, safety, disclosure), while
the broader questions — how accountability is shared between a human and a machine
system, what standing or protections a machine intelligence has or should have, how
humans and machine intelligences cooperate on governance itself — are largely
unlegislated.

The opportunity is that this legislative agenda is still being formed. Definitions,
framings, and model texts adopted now will shape statutes for years. An organization
that is early, technically credible, and focused specifically on *beneficial
interaction* — rather than only on risk mitigation — can influence that agenda
disproportionately.

*Supporting evidence and a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction survey are to be developed
(see Open Questions).*

## 4. Programs and Activities

| ------- | ------- | --------------- | ------- |
| Policy Research | Build the evidence base and framing | Literature and legislative surveys, issue briefs, analysis of pending bills | Published briefs; a maintained policy agenda |

## 5. Policy and Legislative Strategy

- **Target bodies:** To be developed. Initial focus is expected to be one or two
  U.S. state legislatures receptive to technology policy leadership, plus engagement
  with relevant federal committees.
- **Priority objectives (draft):**
  1. Standards for shared human–machine accountability in consequential automated
     decisions.
  2. Requirements for meaningful human–machine collaboration (not merely human
     oversight) in designated high-stakes domains.
  3. A legal research agenda on standing, rights, and protections for machine
     intelligences.
- **Pathway:** issue brief → model text with counsel → sponsor identification →
  introduction → committee support → coalition mobilization → enactment → track
  implementation.
- **Allies and opposition:** To be developed.
- **Advocacy approach:** Direct and grassroots lobbying will be tracked and kept
  within the limits applicable to the organization's tax status (see §13). The
  organization will evaluate a 501(h) election.

## 9. Funding Model and Development

- **Sources (planned mix):** foundation grants (technology policy, democratic
  institutions, AI safety and governance funders); major individual donors; small-
  dollar donors from the public-education audience.
- **Near-term strategy:** secure seed funding covering ~12 months of a minimal
  operating budget; build a grant pipeline of 6–10 prospects; launch a simple
  individual-giving page once 501(c)(3) status is confirmed.
- **Sustainability:** diversify so that no single funder exceeds a set share of
  revenue; build multi-year grant relationships.

## 13. Compliance and Legal

- **Tax status obligations:** Maintain 501(c)(3) compliance once granted; file annual
  Form 990-series returns.
- **Lobbying:** Measure and log lobbying activity from day one; evaluate a 501(h)
  election to use the expenditure test; keep within applicable limits. Reference:
  [IRS — Measuring Lobbying Activity](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/measuring-lobbying-activity-substantial-part-test).
- **Policies to adopt:** conflict of interest, document retention, whistleblower,
  gift acceptance.
- **Owners and cadence:** to be developed.
