## Abstract

The Asimov Project's mission — promoting legislation that fosters the beneficial
interaction of human and machine intelligences — sits within a crowded but unevenly
distributed field of nonprofit organizations. This paper surveys fourteen U.S. and
internationally active nonprofits whose goals overlap with the Asimov Project's,
classifies them by operating mode (research, legislative advocacy, litigation-
adjacent, standards, movement-building) and by policy framing (risk mitigation,
civil rights, transparency, pro-innovation, cooperation), and compares their scale,
geographic focus, and degree of direct legislative engagement. The evidence shows a
mature ecosystem for AI *risk* and *rights* advocacy and a growing one for
pro-innovation advocacy, but only a thin presence at the specific intersection the
Asimov Project occupies: organizations that (a) pursue enacted legislation as their
primary output and (b) frame that legislation around cooperative, mutually beneficial
human–machine outcomes rather than primarily around harm prevention. We recommend the
Asimov Project position itself explicitly in that gap, treat several of the surveyed
organizations as model-legislation and coalition references rather than competitors,
and prioritize early relationships with the two or three whose framing is closest.

## 1. Introduction

Before the Asimov Project commits staff and funding to a legislative agenda, the board
needs a clear picture of who else works in this space: what they do, how they do it,
how large they are, and where their attention is concentrated. That picture serves
three decisions — how to position and differentiate the organization, whom to treat
as a potential ally or coalition partner, and which existing work products (model
bills, policy frameworks, research) the Asimov Project can build on rather than
duplicate.

This paper answers four questions:

1. Which nonprofit organizations have goals that materially overlap with the Asimov
   Project's?
2. How do they operate — research, advocacy, litigation, standards, organizing?
3. How do they frame the underlying policy problem?
4. Where, along both dimensions, is the Asimov Project's niche least crowded?

**In scope:** nonprofit (501(c)(3), 501(c)(4), or non-U.S. equivalent) organizations
active in AI or technology policy, with an emphasis on those that engage with
legislation. **Out of scope:** for-profit consultancies and trade associations,
academic centers without an independent policy program, government bodies (including
AI safety institutes), and think tanks whose AI work is incidental to a broader
portfolio. The survey is a starting map, not a directory; Section 6 states its limits.

## 2. Background

Two developments frame why this landscape matters now.

**State legislation has become the main arena.** As of mid-2026, U.S. states had
enacted on the order of 109 AI-related laws, with Colorado's SB 205 — the first
comprehensive state AI statute — taking effect in February 2026 [16][17]. Most
national advocacy organizations now run state programs.

**A federal preemption fight is active.** Through 2025 and into 2026, Congress
considered measures to preempt or pause state AI laws — first a proposed 10-year
moratorium attached to budget legislation, which the Senate stripped 99–1, and then
the preemption provisions in the bipartisan "Great American Artificial Intelligence
Act of 2026" discussion draft [8][16]. A broad civil-society coalition — including
Public Citizen, Public Knowledge, and the AFL–CIO — mobilized against preemption
[8]. This fight is shaping coalitions and defining which organizations work together.

An organization entering the field in 2026 is therefore entering an active
legislative moment with established players, established coalitions, and an
established (if contested) center of gravity at the state level.

## 3. Methods and Sources

This survey was compiled in September 2026 from public web sources: each
organization's own "about," "work," and "mission" pages; encyclopedic entries
(Wikipedia); nonprofit-transparency databases (GuideStar/Candid, LobbyFacts,
LegiStorm); and contemporaneous reporting from technology-policy outlets
(TechPolicy.Press, GeekWire, IAPP) and general press. Organizations were selected by
searching for nonprofits engaged in AI legislation and AI governance advocacy and
retaining those whose stated goals overlap with the Asimov Project's mission and that
meet the scope rule in Section 1.

For each organization we recorded: legal form (where stated), founding year and
founders, mission statement, primary operating mode, policy framing, geographic
focus, evidence of direct legislative engagement (model bills, sponsorship,
testimony), and any scale or funding figures that appear in public sources. Where a
figure comes from secondary reporting rather than the organization or its filings,
it is marked as such. The evidence base is current to September 2026 and is
predominantly self-reported; Section 6 discusses what that means for confidence.

Every factual claim in Section 4 is tied to a numbered entry in **References**.

## 4. Findings

### 4.1 The organizations

**Legislative advocacy — risk and safety framing**

- **Americans for Responsible Innovation (ARI).** U.S. nonprofit advocating federal
  policy for emerging technologies, founded in late 2023 by former Congressman Brad
  Carson and entrepreneur Eric Gastfriend. Explicitly nonpartisan and independent of
  industry; funded by philanthropy and donor-advised funds; states it takes no money
  from for-profit corporations or non-U.S. entities. Works with lawmakers, national-
  security experts, and civil-society and industry groups to advance bipartisan
  legislation, and has published its own AI governance framework [4].
- **Center for AI Safety Action Fund (CAIS AF).** A 501(c)(4) advocacy arm launched
  in 2024 alongside the research-focused Center for AI Safety. Nonpartisan; frames
  its work around U.S. AI leadership and national-security risk. Co-sponsored
  California's SB 1047 (frontier-model safety testing, incident reporting, shutdown
  capability), which passed the legislature and was vetoed in September 2024 [5].
- **Transparency Coalition (TCAI).** Seattle-based nonprofit founded in 2024 by
  technology executives Jai Jaisimha and Rob Eleveld, focused specifically on AI
  training-data transparency. Publishes model bills and works directly with state
  legislators; contributed to California's Training Data Transparency Act (2024) and
  to the charter of Washington State's AI Task Force [7].
- **Future of Life Institute (FLI).** Established nonprofit (best known for the 2023
  "pause" open letter) whose mission is to steer transformative technology toward
  pro-human outcomes. Runs policy work in Washington, California, Brussels, and at the
  UN; participates in the U.S. AI Safety Institute consortium and promotes AI
  legislation at the state and federal levels. Unusually well-resourced for the
  field: recipient of a large 2021 cryptocurrency donation reported at roughly
  $665 million, plus institutional grants; reported EU advocacy spend around
  €400,000/year [1][2][3].
- **Public First Action.** A 501(c)(4) focused on U.S. AI public policy, advocating
  AI transparency, safeguards, and export controls on advanced AI chips [16][18].

**Legislative advocacy — democracy and consumer-protection framing**

- **Public Citizen.** Long-established (50+ years) national public-interest
  organization with 500,000+ members. Its AI work centers on threats to democratic
  integrity — deepfakes, micro-targeted political manipulation — and it has called
  for a pause on some generative-AI deployment. Active in the anti-preemption
  coalition and files regulatory comments [8].

**Movement-building and youth organizing**

- **Encode (formerly Encode Justice).** Youth-led nonprofit founded July 2020 by
  Sneha Revanur; a chapter network of several hundred students across dozens of
  countries. Frames AI as "not inherently evil" but requiring close regulation and a
  diverse workforce. Co-sponsored SB 1047 and provides an organized youth
  constituency for AI legislation [6][5].

**Research and policy institutes**

- **AI Now Institute.** New York research institute founded 2017 by Kate Crawford and
  Meredith Whittaker; independent since 2022 and does not take corporate/industry
  funding. Focuses on concentration of power, commercial surveillance, labor, and
  accountability; works by identifying "actionable policy windows" with a coalition of
  allies rather than lobbying directly [9].
- **Center for Democracy & Technology (CDT).** Washington internet-policy nonprofit
  whose **AI Governance Lab** develops technically informed regulatory and governance
  proposals, with emphasis on civil rights and the interests of marginalized people.
  Provides expertise into policy and technical standards conversations [10].
- **The Future Society (TFS).** U.S./Europe 501(c)(3) incubated at the Harvard
  Kennedy School in 2014; mission is "aligning AI through better governance." Develops
  and advocates for governance mechanisms from binding law to voluntary standards;
  reports engagement with 8,000+ senior decision-makers and extensive educational
  programming [11].
- **Center for AI and Digital Policy (CAIDP).** Nonprofit whose mission is to ensure
  AI and digital policy promote fundamental rights, democratic institutions, and the
  rule of law. Publishes the annual *AI and Democratic Values Index*, runs an AI
  policy training program, advises governments, and campaigns for instruments such as
  the Council of Europe AI treaty [12].

**Pro-innovation / "abundance" framing**

- **Abundance Institute.** Mission-driven nonprofit working to create the cultural and
  policy environment for emerging technologies (AI, energy, health) to "germinate,
  develop, and thrive." On AI it advocates a pro-innovation policy environment and has
  worked on federal tools to address what it calls state regulatory overreach [13].

**Cooperative / collective-intelligence framing**

- **Cooperative AI Foundation (CAIF).** Charitable entity backed by a $15 million
  commitment from Macroscopic Ventures; mission is to improve the "cooperative
  intelligence" of advanced AI for the benefit of all, on the premise that major
  global problems are problems of cooperation among humans, machines, and
  institutions. Primarily funds and convenes research [14].
- **Collective Intelligence Project (CIP).** Incubator for new governance models for
  transformative technology; develops "collective intelligence capabilities" —
  decision-making processes and institutions that expand a group's capacity to
  cooperate toward shared goals. Runs pilots and research, some jointly with CAIF
  [15].

### 4.3 Patterns

1. **The field is dense on risk and rights, sparse on cooperation.** Ten of the
   fourteen organizations frame the policy problem primarily as preventing harm
   (safety, catastrophic risk, civil-rights violations, democratic manipulation,
   opacity). One frames it as accelerating benefit (Abundance). Two frame it around
   cooperation and shared goals (CAIF, CIP) — but neither pursues legislation.
2. **Legislative advocacy and cooperative framing do not currently coincide.** The
   organizations that actually move bills (ARI, CAIS AF, Transparency Coalition,
   Encode, Public First Action) all frame their work around risk, transparency, or
   competitiveness. The organizations that frame their work around beneficial
   human–machine cooperation (CAIF, CIP) are research and convening bodies.
3. **The state level is where legislative work happens**, and organizations with
   published model bills and legislator relationships (Transparency Coalition, CAIS
   AF) have had the most concrete enacted-law impact.
4. **Scale varies by orders of magnitude** — from youth chapter networks and small
   founder-led nonprofits to FLI's nine-figure endowment — and reliable budget
   figures are mostly not public (Section 6).
5. **Coalitions are already forming around the preemption fight**, which is a
   ready-made entry point for a new organization to build relationships.

## 5. Discussion

**Positioning.** The Asimov Project's stated mission — legislation for the *beneficial
interaction* of human and machine intelligences — is not well served by any single
existing organization. The closest in *framing* (Cooperative AI Foundation, Collective
Intelligence Project) do not do legislative work; the closest in *method* (ARI, CAIS
Action Fund, Transparency Coalition) do not use the cooperative frame. This is an
argument for entering the field with the cooperative-legislation position stated
explicitly and repeatedly, because it is genuinely differentiated rather than a
crowded "me too."

**Competitor or complement?** Most of these organizations are better treated as
complements. Transparency Coalition and CAIS Action Fund demonstrate a working model
for state-level model-bill advocacy that the Asimov Project can learn from directly.
CDT's AI Governance Lab, The Future Society, and CAIDP produce governance analysis
the Asimov Project can cite rather than reproduce. CAIF and CIP are the natural
research partners for the "beneficial interaction" evidence base. Encode offers an
organized constituency. The main risk of direct competition is for the same
philanthropic funding pool (see below).

**Implications for the business plan.** This survey supports three refinements to the
[business plan](../Business%20Plan/Asimov%20Business%20Plan.md): (1) §5 Policy and
Legislative Strategy should name the cooperative-legislation niche as the explicit
differentiator; (2) §6 Stakeholders should list Transparency Coalition, CAIS Action
Fund, CAIF, CIP, and Encode as first-tier engagement targets; (3) §9 Funding should
acknowledge that ARI, FLI, and the CAIS entities draw on the same AI-governance
philanthropy (Open Philanthropy, Survival and Flourishing Fund, and similar), which
both validates the funding thesis and signals competition for it.

**Counter-reading.** One could argue the cooperative frame is a distinction without a
difference — that "beneficial interaction" legislation will in practice look like the
accountability and transparency bills others already push, so the Asimov Project
should simply join an existing coalition rather than stand up a new organization. That
argument has force in the short term. The response is that framing shapes which bills
get written at all: none of the surveyed organizations is currently drafting
legislation about *how humans and machine intelligences share decisions and
governance*, as opposed to how humans constrain machines. Whether that gap justifies
a standalone organization is precisely the board's decision to make.

## 6. Limitations

- **Self-reported evidence.** Most organizational descriptions come from the
  organizations' own websites and press. Missions are aspirational statements, not
  audited descriptions of activity.
- **No financial due diligence.** IRS Form 990 filings were not reviewed. Budget,
  staff-count, and funding figures are sparse, are drawn from secondary reporting
  where they appear, and are not comparable across organizations.
- **Selection bias.** The set was assembled from web searches in a single month.
  Organizations with weaker search presence, very new entrants, and most
  state-specific groups are underrepresented. International bodies outside the U.S./EU
  orbit are largely absent.
- **Framing is interpreted.** The "policy framing" and "primary mode" classifications
  are the authors' reading of public materials and could be contested by the
  organizations themselves.
- **A fast-moving field.** Coalition membership, leadership, and legislative posture
  change quickly; the preemption fight in particular may look different within
  months.
- **No conflict of interest** is known between the Asimov Project and any surveyed
  organization; none was investigated.

The findings can bear the weight of positioning and relationship-prioritization
decisions. They cannot bear the weight of funding-competition or partnership
commitments without the follow-up research in Section 8.

## 7. Recommendations

1. **Adopt the cooperative-legislation position explicitly.** State the Asimov
   Project's differentiator — legislation about beneficial, cooperative human–machine
   interaction, not primarily about constraining machines — in the mission-facing
   language of the website, the business plan §5, and all funder materials. *(Ties to
   findings 4.3.1–4.3.2.)*
2. **Treat peers as complements; build a five-organization engagement shortlist.**
   Prioritize early contact with Transparency Coalition and CAIS Action Fund (method
   models), Cooperative AI Foundation and Collective Intelligence Project (framing
   allies and research partners), and Encode (constituency). *(Ties to 4.3.2, 5.)*
3. **Reuse, don't rebuild.** Before drafting original policy analysis, check whether
   CDT's AI Governance Lab, The Future Society, or CAIDP have already covered the
   ground, and cite them. *(Ties to 4.3, 5.)*
4. **Enter through the preemption coalition.** Use the active federal preemption
   fight as the low-cost first venue for building legislator and peer relationships,
   without making preemption the organization's identity. *(Ties to 2, 4.3.5.)*
5. **Map the funding overlap before the first grant cycle.** Confirm which funders
   support ARI, FLI, and the CAIS entities and plan for differentiation in those
   applications. *(Ties to 5; feeds business plan §9 and issue #20.)*
6. **Decision for the board.** Given findings 4.3.1–4.3.2, decide whether the
   cooperative-legislation gap justifies a standalone organization or argues for
   operating as a program within an existing coalition. This paper frames the
   decision; it does not make it.

## 8. Open Questions and Next Research

- **Financial benchmarking.** Pull Form 990s for the U.S. organizations to compare
  budgets, staffing, and lobbying expenditures. *(Candidate follow-up issue.)*
- **Funder map.** Identify the major AI-governance philanthropies and which surveyed
  organizations they fund. *(Feeds business plan issue #20.)*
- **State-group survey.** Catalog state-specific AI advocacy groups and legislator
  champions in the two or three target jurisdictions. *(Feeds business plan issue
  #21.)*
- **Model-bill review.** Assess published model bills (Transparency Coalition and
  others) as structural precedents for a cooperative-interaction bill. *(Feeds a
  future white paper.)*
- **International comparison.** Extend the survey to non-U.S./EU organizations and to
  the OECD/UN/Council of Europe instruments.
- **Framing test.** Interview two or three peer organizations to check whether the
  "cooperative vs. constraining" distinction reads as real to practitioners
  (addresses the Section 5 counter-reading).
