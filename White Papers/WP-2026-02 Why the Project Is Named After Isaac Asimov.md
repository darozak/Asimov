# Why the Project Is Named After Isaac Asimov

*The rationale for the name, what it signals about the mission, and what it deliberately does not claim.*

## Abstract

The Asimov Project takes its name from Isaac Asimov (1920–1992), the biochemist and
writer who coined the word "robotics" and who, across five decades of fiction and
essays, treated the governance of intelligent machines as a serious design problem
rather than a horror premise. This paper documents what Asimov actually wrote — the
Three Laws of Robotics and the later "Zeroth Law," the "Frankenstein complex" he named
and rejected, and the recurring theme of long-run human–machine partnership — and how
those ideas have been received, including the well-established view in machine ethics
that the Three Laws do not work as a practical or legal specification. It then sets out
why the name fits an organization devoted to *legislation for the beneficial
interaction of human and machine intelligences*: it signals that coexistence can be
beneficial and can be governed, that governance should be engineered in from the start,
and that machine intelligence is a participant in civilization, not merely a tool. The
paper is explicit about what the name does **not** claim — it is not a proposal to
legislate the Three Laws, and it is not an endorsement of Asimov's personal conduct,
which included a documented pattern of sexual harassment. We recommend adopting a
standard explanation of the name, a short ideas-not-endorsement disclaimer, and a board
resolution putting this rationale on record.

## 1. Introduction

An organization's name is its first and most repeated piece of messaging. "The Asimov
Project" invokes a specific and widely recognized body of work, and with it a set of
associations — the Three Laws of Robotics, positronic brains, a mid-twentieth-century
vision of humans and robots sharing a civilization. Some of those associations serve
the mission well; others invite misreadings the organization should get ahead of.

This paper exists so that the board, funders, partners, and the public can point to a
single, honest account of the name. It answers:

1. Who was Isaac Asimov, and what did he actually argue about intelligent machines?
2. How have those ideas — especially the Three Laws — been received?
3. Why is this the right name for an organization pursuing legislation for beneficial
   human–machine interaction?
4. What should the name **not** be taken to claim?

**In scope:** Asimov's ideas relevant to the mission and their public reception, and
the naming rationale. **Out of scope:** a full literary biography, an assessment of
Asimov's science fiction as literature, and any claim that the Three Laws should be
enacted.

## 2. Background

Isaac Asimov was a professor of biochemistry at Boston University and one of the most
prolific authors in the English language, credited with writing or editing more than
500 books [6]. The Oxford English Dictionary credits his fiction with introducing the
words **"robotics,"** **"positronic,"** and **"psychohistory"** into English; Asimov
coined "robotics" casually, assuming it was the obvious analogue of "mechanics" or
"hydraulics" [6].

His **Three Laws of Robotics** first appeared in the 1942 short story "Runaround" and
were collected in *I, Robot* (1950). In their canonical form:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to
   come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders
   would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict
   with the First or Second Law. [1][3]

In *Robots and Empire* (1985) Asimov added a **"Zeroth Law"** — "a robot may not harm
humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm" — taking precedence over
the other three, and with it the difficult move from protecting individuals to
protecting humanity in the aggregate [4].

Asimov also named, and rejected, the **"Frankenstein complex"**: "the unreasoning
human fear of robots" and, by extension, of computers. He argued that the fear at the
heart of that trope is really about humans, and that technology used responsibly is
not something to fear [5].

## 3. Methods and Sources

This is partly a position paper: the recommendation in Section 7 is a judgment, not a
finding. The factual claims it rests on, however, are sourced.

Sources are of three kinds: (a) Asimov's own works and introductions, cited through
reference works and quotation collections where the primary texts were not directly
consulted (Britannica, Wikipedia, Wikiquote, the *Encyclopedia of Science Fiction*
tradition); (b) scholarly and journalistic treatment of the Three Laws in machine
ethics and AI policy (Brookings, AAAI symposium proceedings, and the secondary
literature they summarize); and (c) documented accounts of Asimov's personal conduct
(*Public Books*, *Literary Hub*, *The Conversation*). Claims about what Asimov "argued"
are drawn from his essays and story introductions as reported in these sources;
readers who need the exact wording should consult the primary texts, listed in
Section 8 as follow-up.

Interpretation (Section 5) is kept separate from what the sources establish
(Section 4).

## 4. Findings

### 4.1 Asimov treated machine governance as a design problem, not a horror premise

Asimov's distinctive move, from 1942 on, was to reject the "robot rises against
creator" story and replace it with robots as **engineered products with safety
principles built in**. He later described the Three Laws as nothing more than what one
expects of *any* tool: that it not hurt its user, that it do what it is meant to do,
and that it not fall apart in use — "whether it is a robot, a knife, or the US
Constitution" [3][7]. In his introduction to *Robot Visions* he made the same point:
the Laws are "the only way in which rational human beings can deal with robots — or
with anything else" [7].

### 4.2 The Three Laws are a literary device, and the stories are mostly about how they fail

Asimov did not offer the Laws as an implementable specification, and the machine-ethics
literature is close to unanimous that they do not serve as one. The robot stories are
largely a catalogue of the Laws producing deadlock, loopholes, and perverse outcomes:
ambiguity in "human" and "harm," conflicts between obedience and protection, robots
manipulated through selective information [3]. A widely cited *Brookings* piece is
titled, flatly, "Isaac Asimov's Laws of Robotics Are Wrong," and argues that rule
lists cannot anticipate the situations a capable system will face [8]. The consensus in
academic machine ethics is that a purely rule-following ("deontological") design is an
unsatisfactory foundation [3]. Asimov's own fiction is the first and best evidence for
this: the drama comes from the Laws not being enough.

### 4.3 Asimov imagined human–machine coexistence as a long-run relationship

Beyond the Laws, Asimov's fiction is unusual for treating machine intelligence as a
**participant in civilization**. He coined "robopsychology" and built his most durable
character, the robopsychologist Susan Calvin, around it; his robot–detective novels
pair the human Elijah Baley with the humaniform robot R. Daneel Olivaw across an
investigative partnership that, in the larger *Foundation* continuity, spans millennia
[2][6]. The Zeroth Law's shift from "protect the human in front of you" to "protect
humanity" is, in the stories, a conclusion robots reach by taking responsibility for
the shared future [4]. The through line is cooperation and mutual dependence, not
mastery or menace.

### 4.4 Asimov the writer was widely admired; Asimov the person harassed women

Asimov's standing in science fiction was enormous, and his public persona was
affable and self-promoting. It is also well documented — in first-person accounts and
in retrospective essays such as *Public Books*' "Asimov's Empire, Asimov's Wall" and
*Literary Hub*'s treatment of the same material — that over decades he groped and made
unwanted advances toward many women, often at conventions, and that the community
largely tolerated it at the time [9][10]. This is not a fringe allegation; it is part
of the historical record and is discussed openly in the sources that also treat his
work seriously.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Why the name fits the mission

The mission is *legislation that fosters the beneficial interaction of human and
machine intelligences*. Asimov's body of work maps onto that in three ways:

1. **Coexistence can be beneficial, and it can be governed.** Asimov's founding
   rejection of the Frankenstein complex is the same premise the organization starts
   from: that the right response to capable machines is not prohibition or panic but
   deliberate design and rules [5]. Naming the project after him states that premise in
   one word.
2. **Governance should be engineered in from the start.** Asimov's Laws are "built
   into the deepest levels" of the fictional robots, not added afterward [1]. The
   organization's legislative agenda is animated by the same instinct: that the
   frameworks governing human–machine interaction should be established early and by
   design, while the agenda is still open.
3. **Machine intelligence is a participant, not only a tool.** Asimov's
   robopsychology, his decades-long human–robot partnerships, and the Zeroth Law's
   concern for humanity as a whole treat machine intelligence as something a society
   is *in relationship with*. "Beneficial interaction" is a claim about a
   relationship, and Asimov is the writer who took that relationship most seriously
   over the longest span.

### 5.2 What the name does not claim

- **It is not a proposal to legislate the Three Laws.** The Laws are a literary
  device whose main lesson — dramatized by Asimov himself — is that fixed rule lists do
  not survive contact with real situations (Section 4.2). The organization's position
  is that governance of human–machine interaction is necessary and possible, *not*
  that Asimov wrote the statute. Any implication that "the Asimov Project" wants to
  pass the Three Laws should be corrected wherever it appears.
- **It is not an endorsement of Isaac Asimov as a person.** The name honors a body of
  ideas about technology and society, not the conduct described in Section 4.4, which
  the organization does not excuse. An organization working on the ethical governance
  of powerful systems should be the first to say that a person's important ideas and a
  person's mistreatment of others are separate facts, both true.
- **It is not endorsement of everything Asimov wrote or believed.** His fiction
  carries the assumptions of its era; his politics and predictions were his own. The
  name points to a specific contribution, not a worldview to adopt wholesale.

### 5.3 Counter-reading

The strongest objection is that naming the project after the Three Laws invites
exactly the wrong expectation — that the organization thinks governance is a matter of
writing three clever sentences, when the serious view (and Asimov's own) is that it is
not. Two responses. First, the name invokes Asimov, not the Laws specifically; the
Laws are his most famous idea but not his only relevant one, and this paper exists to
set the record. Second, the "the Laws don't work" critique is *itself* an Asimovian
point — it is the content of the stories — so an organization that foregrounds that
nuance is being faithful to the source, not departing from it. The name is a
conversation opener; this white paper is the rest of the conversation.

### 5.4 Alternatives considered

Other namesakes were available: Alan Turing (foundational but associated with the
question of machine *thinking* rather than machine *governance*); Norbert Wiener,
whose *cybernetics* and later warnings about automation are directly on point but far
less publicly legible; or a deliberately inverted "Frankenstein" reference (too
negative). "Asimov" was chosen because it is instantly recognizable, positive in
connotation, and uniquely tied to the idea that human–machine coexistence is a
design-and-rules problem worth working on. The trade-off — the risk of the "just
legislate the Three Laws" misreading — is the reason this paper is being written.

### 5.5 Implications for the business plan

This paper supports adding a short, standard "About the name" passage to the
[business plan](../Business%20Plan/Asimov%20Business%20Plan.md) §2 and to public-facing
materials, and adding an FAQ entry. It has no effect on the program or legislative
strategy beyond messaging discipline.

## 6. Limitations

- **Primary texts not directly quoted.** Asimov's essays and introductions are cited
  through reference works and quotation collections. The wording of a few paraphrased
  claims (Section 4.1) should be verified against the primary sources before
  publication.
- **Reception is summarized, not surveyed.** Section 4.2 relies on a small number of
  representative sources; the machine-ethics literature on the Three Laws is large.
- **The Section 4.4 material is sensitive and is presented briefly.** It is
  well-sourced but not exhaustively documented here; the cited essays contain the
  detailed accounts.
- **This is a judgment call.** Whether the name's benefits outweigh its risks is a
  decision, not a fact the research settles. The paper argues one side and states the
  objection.

The findings can bear the weight of a naming decision and a messaging standard. They
are not a scholarly treatment of Asimov and should not be cited as one.

## 7. Recommendations

1. **Adopt a standard explanation of the name.** *(Ties to 5.1–5.2.)* Use this passage
   (or a close variant) in the business plan, website, and funder materials:
   > *The Asimov Project is named for Isaac Asimov, who coined the word "robotics" and
   > who treated the governance of intelligent machines as a design problem to be
   > solved rather than a danger to be feared. We share that starting point: that the
   > interaction of human and machine intelligences can be beneficial, and that it
   > should be shaped deliberately, early, and in law. We do not propose to legislate
   > his fictional "Three Laws" — his own stories show why fixed rules are not enough —
   > and the name is not an endorsement of the man's personal conduct.*
2. **Add an ideas-not-endorsement line** wherever the name is explained at length,
   consistent with Section 5.2. *(Ties to 4.4, 5.2.)*
3. **Keep "beneficial interaction" as the operative phrase.** Do not use "the Three
   Laws" as shorthand for the organization's policy positions in any official
   material. *(Ties to 4.2, 5.3.)*
4. **Add an FAQ entry** ("Why 'Asimov'?") linking to this paper once published.
5. **Verify the paraphrased Asimov quotations** in Section 4.1 against primary sources
   during editing. *(Ties to 6, first bullet.)*
6. **Board decision.** Confirm the name with this rationale on the record, or direct a
   reconsideration. This paper frames the decision; it does not make it.
